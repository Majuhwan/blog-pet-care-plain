A Daily Care Checklist for New Pet Owners

A daily and seasonal pet care checklist built from official AVMA and ASPCA resources, covering vaccines, hygiene, and emergency readiness.

Pet Care, PlainlyOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

This article is general information, not veterinary advice. Talk to a licensed veterinarian about your specific pet's health needs.

Once the first few weeks of pet ownership settle into a routine, it helps to have a simple reference for what should happen daily, what should happen on a recurring schedule, and what should be set up once and then checked periodically. The checklist below is built directly from topics covered by the AVMA, the ASPCA, and the CDC's Healthy Pets Healthy People program.

Daily

Handwashing after specific contact points. The CDC's guidance lists concrete moments, not just "wash your hands sometimes": after touching or playing with your pet, after feeding it or handling its food, after handling its habitat or equipment, after cleaning up after it, and after removing soiled clothes or shoes.

Litter box cleaning, if you have a cat. The CDC specifically recommends daily cleaning "to lower the chances of exposure to harmful parasites."

Picking up after your dog. The CDC notes dog and cat waste "can contain parasites and germs that can be harmful to people," and recommends always removing it from your yard and public places using a bag, disposing of it properly.

Recurring, but not daily

Vaccinations. The ASPCA's pet care resources include a dedicated section on vaccinations, describing them as "one of the easiest ways to help [a pet] live a long, healthy life." Vaccine schedules vary by pet type, age, and local requirements, which is specifically why this is a veterinary conversation rather than a fixed universal schedule.

Flea and tick checks. The ASPCA's resources specifically call out the importance of recognizing "the signs and symptoms of fleas and ticks," framing this as an ongoing awareness task, not a one-time treatment.

Spay or neuter, if not already done. The ASPCA frames this directly as population control: "Spaying or neutering your pet helps control the pet overpopulation crisis," and its resources include a searchable database of low-cost spay/neuter providers for owners for whom cost is a barrier.

Set up once, then revisit periodically

An emergency care plan. The ASPCA's resources state plainly: "It's crucial to have an emergency plan in place, before you need it." This is explicitly framed as advance preparation, not something to figure out in the moment of an actual emergency.

A disaster preparedness plan. Separately from day-to-day emergencies, the ASPCA maintains dedicated guidance on disaster preparedness for pets, on the logic that "the best thing you can do for yourself and your pets is to be prepared in case of disaster," covering situations like evacuations where pets need to travel or be sheltered alongside the household.

A lost-pet action plan. The ASPCA's resources specifically recommend knowing the steps to "take swift action" if a pet goes missing, which is far more effective decided calmly in advance than improvised during the stress of a pet actually being lost.

Seasonal

The ASPCA maintains season-specific guidance, with winter safety specifically named in its general pet care resources, including tips from its Poison Control team aimed at keeping pets "safe, happy and healthy during the winter months." If you live somewhere with meaningful seasonal weather changes, checking in on seasonal-specific guidance at the start of each season is a reasonable, low-effort habit to add to this checklist.

Who to involve if you're unsure

For any of these items, both the AVMA and ASPCA point back to the same resource repeatedly: a licensed veterinarian. The AVMA's own framing of responsible pet ownership keeps a veterinary relationship at the center of this checklist, not as a last resort when something goes wrong, but as the ongoing reference point for vaccine timing, flea/tick prevention choices, and spay/neuter timing specific to your pet's breed, age, and health history.

A simple weekly review

Rather than trying to remember every item above from memory every day, a short weekly review works well in practice:

  1. Daily habits check themselves naturally (handwashing, litter box, waste pickup) once established as routine.
  2. Once a week, glance at your pet's vaccine and flea/tick prevention schedule to see if anything is due soon.
  3. Once a season, check the ASPCA's current seasonal guidance for anything specific to the upcoming weather.
  4. Once a year (or whenever you move, travel, or your household changes), revisit your emergency, disaster, and lost-pet plans to make sure they still reflect your current situation.

Key takeaways

  • Daily tasks (handwashing at specific contact points, litter box cleaning, waste pickup) are the foundation and the easiest to make habitual.
  • Vaccinations, flea/tick prevention, and spay/neuter timing are recurring but not daily, and should be set on a schedule discussed with a veterinarian rather than guessed at.
  • Emergency, disaster, and lost-pet plans should be set up once, in advance, specifically because the ASPCA's own guidance frames advance preparation as more effective than improvising during an actual crisis.
  • A veterinarian should be the central ongoing reference point for anything specific to your pet's breed, age, or health history, not just a resource for when something has already gone wrong.
This article is for general information only and is not veterinary advice. Ask your veterinarian about your own pet.
